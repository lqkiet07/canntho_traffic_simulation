Introduction

Traﬃc congestion remains a critical challenge for sustainable urban development,
particularly in developing countries such as Vietnam, where mixed traﬃc ﬂows
are dominated by motorcycles that typically account for 85% to 90% of the total
traﬃc volume [5,10]. As documented in the literature, the non-lane-based, freeﬁltering, and swarming behaviors of dense motorcycle streams render traditional
car-centric signal control strategies highly ineﬀective [14]. Unlike motor vehicles
that follow structured queuing patterns, motorcycles ﬁll available road gaps continuously, causing traditional discrete vehicle-counting methods to produce systematic inaccuracies. This leads to functional limitations in existing ﬁxed-time
signal plans and a measurable degradation of actual intersection capacity [6].
Although deploying real-time adaptive signal control frameworkssuch as the
Max Pressure (MP) architectureis increasingly urgent, direct ﬁeld trials face
substantial barriers owing to limited physical sensor infrastructure and the high
risk of cascading gridlocks during controller calibration [12,15]. To bypass these
practical constraints, a Digital Twin (DT) architecture oﬀers a viable solution
by providing a safe, high-ﬁdelity virtual testing environment prior to real-world
deployment [4,1]. By applying Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) on the open-source
GAMA multi-agent simulation platform [11,8], this study constructs a virtual
testbed designed to replicate complex mixed-traﬃc interactions and emergent
swarming behaviors under controlled conditions.
For the control strategy, the Cycle-Based Max Pressure (C-MP) algorithm is
selected due to its proven ability to maximize network throughput through decentralized queue balancing while satisfying industrial ﬁxed-cycle constraints [3].

To overcome the vulnerability of vision-based pipelines to perspective distortion,
vehicle occlusion, and minor camera displacements that misalign rigid Regions of
Interest (ROIs), this study proposes replacing discrete vehicle counts with a continuous state-space metric termed Continuous Area Occupancy (CAO), drawing
on spatial saturation principles adapted from aerial surveillance concepts [2].
Leveraging YOLOv8 semantic segmentation, the proposed framework isolates
the drivable road surface dynamically, thereby providing accurate, infrastructureresilient spatial pressure values as inputs to the C-MP controller [13].
The experimental methodology encompasses two core phases: oﬄine computer vision processing for baseline validation, and large-scale virtual network
evaluation within the GAMA-based Digital Twin. Through a comparative analysis of the proposed CAO-based C-MP, the traditional vehicle-counting C-MP,
and standard ﬁxed-time control, this study benchmarks average delay, queue
length, and throughput. The results demonstrate the operational superiority and
structural resilience of the proposed system under dense, motorcycle-dominated
traﬃc conditions.

2

Literature Review

The ﬁeld of network-level traﬃc signal control has evolved considerably, progressing from rigid, centralized heuristics toward dynamic, distributed feedback strategies. In particular, the Max Pressure (MP) control framework has
attracted considerable research attention due to its mathematical capacity to
stabilize traﬃc networks under high demand conditions [12]. Subsequent studies
extended this foundational theory by incorporating localized storage constraints,
boundary capacities, and turning movement dynamics to better accommodate
varied network topologies [9]. To facilitate practical ﬁeld deployment, the CycleBased Max Pressure (C-MP) framework was introduced, allocating green time
splits proportionally at cycle boundaries and rendering the algorithm compatible with standard industrial signal controllers while preserving network stability
[3]. In parallel, high-ﬁdelity microscopic simulation platformsnotably the GAMA
multi-agent simulation platformhave been widely adopted to replicate complex
urban traﬃc dynamics and evaluate advanced control strategies in a controlled
environment prior to ﬁeld implementation [11,8]. However, these conventional
frameworks are generally bounded by the assumption of structured, lane-based
environments and homogeneous vehicle proﬁles. In non-lane-based networks, the
linear relationship between discrete vehicle counts and physical intersection pressure frequently deviates from actual conditions, limiting the ability to accurately
reﬂect real urban traﬃc states.
Concurrently, the research community has increasingly addressed the complexities of mixed traﬃc streams common in developing nations, where highly
dense, non-lane-based motorcycle traﬃc constitutes a dominant share of urban
transport ﬂows [5,10]. Early approaches applied static Passenger Car Equivalents
(PCE) or Passenger Car Units (PCU) to estimate road capacity and convert heterogeneous ﬂows into a standardized metric [6]. While these conversion methods

provide a structured adjustment for vehicle size diﬀerences, they face inherent limitations in highly dynamic, space-ﬁlling environments. Unlike passenger
cars, dense motorcycle streams frequently exhibit non-linear swarming behaviors and aggregate compactly within any available road space, substantially inﬂuencing overall intersection dynamics [14]. Consequently, traditional discrete
counting methods may not adequately capture the continuous spatial saturation
of such ﬂows, leading to inaccurate pressure estimations and suboptimal phase
allocations. To reﬁne these estimations, recent works have explored integrating
heuristics with microscopic simulation to optimize signalized intersections under
motorcycle-dominated conditions [13].
On the technological side, the development of Digital Twins for intersection
management has accelerated, oﬀering virtual environments for real-time trafﬁc monitoring, performance evaluation, and systematic controller calibration
[4,1]. Within these frameworks, adaptive traﬃc control systems have frequently
leveraged deep learning object detectors, including the YOLO model family,
alongside Digital Twin architectures to automate data extraction within predeﬁned geometric Regions of Interest (ROIs) [7]. Nonetheless, vision-based estimation pipelines encounter practical challenges under real-world infrastructure
constraints. Urban cameras are often mounted at oblique angles that introduce
perspective distortion and inter-vehicle occlusion. Additionally, environmental
factors such as wind or structural vibrations can cause minor camera displacements, resulting in misalignment between the rigid geometric ROI and the physical road boundaries. Such misalignment can introduce systematic errors into the
data pipeline, aﬀecting the reliability of downstream controller state estimations
[15].
To address these constraints, this study proposes an alternative continuous
state-space formulation termed Continuous Area Occupancy (CAO), drawing on
the concept of spatial occupancy previously observed in aerial traﬃc monitoring [2]. Rather than relying on discrete object detection within ﬁxed geometric
boundaries, the proposed framework employs YOLOv8 semantic segmentation
to dynamically isolate the actual drivable road surface. By computing the pixel
ratio of vehicle footprints relative to the dynamically segmented roadway, this
method measures spatial saturation continuously. This approach reduces dependency on rigid ROIs and decreases susceptibility to perspective distortion or
minor camera displacements, thereby oﬀering a more robust state estimation
alternative for dense, mixed traﬃc conditions.

Methodology

The methodological framework of this study is organized into three sequential
subsections. The ﬁrst subsection describes the overall system architecture, illustrating the closed-loop co-simulation workﬂow between the GAMA-based Digital
Twin platform and the signal control module. The second subsection introduces
the YOLOv8 semantic segmentation pipeline for computer vision data extraction, detailing how perspective information is utilized to estimate the Continuous

Area Occupancy (CAO) ratio from virtual camera streams. The third subsection
formulates the adaptive C-MP optimization model, which dynamically recalibrates the signal cycle length and green time splits under strict technical and

3.1

System Architecture

To realize the Digital Twin model supporting adaptive signal control for mixed
traﬃc, the operational workﬂow is designed as a closed-loop framework consisting of eight sequential steps across two core layers: Traﬃc Simulation and
the Signal Control Algorithm. The architectural interaction and data exchange
between these layers are illustrated in Figure 1.

Fig. 1. Systematic ﬂowchart of the spatial-occupancy adaptive signal control architecture across traﬃc simulation and control layers.

Step 1. The system extracts base map data from the OpenStreetMap (OSM)
platform for the designated case study area in Can Tho City, Vietnam. QGIS is
subsequently used to standardize the geometric dimensions of the roadways according to real-world scales, precisely mapping the coordinate network, adjacent
buildings, intersection geometries, and the spatial distribution of signal heads.
This spatial dataset is then integrated into the GAMA multi-agent simulation
environment to establish a real-time visual rendering pipeline.
Step 2. Traﬃc characteristics in a developing urban area such as Can Tho are
highly chaotic, characterized by non-lane-based, free-ﬂowing movements. Based
on ﬁeld data collected via surveillance cameras at the target intersection during
peak hours, the vehicle composition is conﬁgured with a dominant motorcycle
ratio: 84% motorcycles, 11% passenger cars, and 5% trucks. To stress-test the
system’s capacity boundary and resilience, three incremental traﬃc demand levels are applied: low (400 vph), medium (900 vph), and high (1400 vph). Since

wide-area camera data across the entire network is unavailable, turning ratio
distributions are modeled via two predeﬁned routing conﬁgurations: Balanced
Turning Ratios, where ﬂows are distributed evenly with 70% through, 15% leftturn, and 15% right-turn movements; and Unbalanced Turning Ratios, which
simulate severe asymmetric directional pressures with 50% through, 35% leftturn, and 15% right-turn movements.
Step 3. Virtual camera nodes are deployed at each intersection approach
within the simulation environment to capture perspective visual streams. The
Regions of Interest are conﬁgured dynamically, where the spatial length of each
ROI is determined based on the actual physical distance from the adjacent upstream intersection to the stop line of the current approach.
Step 4. From the visual streams obtained in Step 3, the Continuous Area
Occupancy (CAO) ratio is extracted to accurately reﬂect the dynamic saturation state of the road surface, replacing discrete vehicle counts. This metric is
subsequently used to determine the optimal adaptive cycle length (T ) for the
intersection at the current control interval. This cycle length then serves as a
direct input to the phase allocation algorithm in Step 6.
Step 5. The system evaluates the elapsed time of the active green phase
against a predeﬁned safety threshold of gmin = 10 seconds, established based on
standardized urban traﬃc safety regulations [3] to guarantee suﬃcient pedestrian crossing time and prevent vehicles from entering the dilemma zone. If the
condition is not satisﬁed, the system rejects phase termination and reverts to
Step 2 to maintain the current simulation state. If the condition is satisﬁed, the
process advances to Step 6.
Step 6. The enhanced Cycle-Based Max Pressure (C-MP) algorithm is activated to generate the optimal signal timing plan. The core contribution lies
in the reformulation of the pressure weight (w): instead of counting discrete vehicles as in the standard C-MP formulation (Eq. (2)), the system incorporates
the continuous spatial occupancy ratio ϕ (Eq. (3)) derived from the YOLOv8
semantic segmentation model. Based on these spatial pressure weights, Eq. (3),
Eq. (4), and Eq. (5) are solved sequentially to yield the optimal green splits and
phase allocations.
Step 7. The green splits derived from Step 6 are validated against two system
constraints (Eq. (6) and Eq. (7)): a maximum green time ceiling (gmax ) to prevent
starvation on opposing approaches, and a boundary constraint ensuring that the
sum of all phase durations equals the adaptive cycle length T computed in Step 4.
If either constraint is violated, the proposed conﬁguration is discarded to prevent
network destabilization, and the loop reverts to Step 2. If both constraints are
satisﬁed, the approved conﬁguration is forwarded to the actuation layer.
Step 8. Upon clearing all constraint ﬁlters, the system applies Eq. (8) to
convert the optimal phase ratios into precise green durations in seconds. Execution proceeds through two parallel actions: (8.a) Execute Phase Change issues
the phase transition commands and transmits the control packets to the signal
controller; and (8.b) Signal Head Update dynamically renders the corresponding
signal states (Green/Yellow/Red) onto the physical signal structures within the

4

Experimental Framework

To implement a safe sandbox testing environment for the enhanced C-MP signal control algorithm, this study adopts the Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) approach, which enables traﬃc entities to be modeled as autonomous agents possessing individual behavioral attributes and the capacity for non-linear interactions with the environment. The open-source GAMA multi-agent simulation
platform [11,8] is selected as the microscopic simulation core for the proposed
Digital Twin system.

4.1

Simulation Set-up

The microscopic simulation environment is constructed on the GAMA multiagent simulation platform, utilizing spatial data from OpenStreetMap (OSM)
to accurately digitize the geometric layout of the target intersection in Can Tho
City. To ensure high spatial ﬁdelity prior to importation, the open-source QGIS
software is used to process, align, and reﬁne the raw geodata. The resulting simulation layout, which captures the complex intersection geometry, is visualized
in Fig. 2.

Fig. 2. Spatial layout of the target intersection processed via QGIS and imported into
the GAMA simulation platform.

To capture the non-lane-based swarming behavior of mixed traﬃc ﬂows, the
simulation integrates customized Virtual Detection Zones (VDZs) in place of
traditional physical lanes. Within this ABM framework, the environmentcomprising the road network, traﬃc signal controllers, and detection zonesdynamically interacts with heterogeneous vehicle agents (motorcycles, passenger cars,
and trucks). Operating as a closed-loop Digital Twin system, real-time Continuous Area Occupancy (CAO) values enable the C-MP algorithm to update signal
timings at each control cycle. The GIS-based modular structure ensures transferability, providing a scalable template for district-level adaptive signal control
without requiring modiﬁcation of the core mathematical logic.
